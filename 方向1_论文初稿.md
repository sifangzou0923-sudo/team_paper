## Abstract

Abrasive air jets (AAJs) are an emerging means of rock and coal breakage, permeability enhancement, and assisted tunnelling in deep mining. Predicting breakage outcomes (erosion depth, erosion volume, erosion rate) from jet, abrasive, nozzle, and target parameters is essential for the rapid adjustment of working parameters in the field, yet two existing routes fall short: semi-empirical formulas generalize poorly beyond the conditions they were fitted on, and physics-based CFD–DEM simulations are too expensive for design-space exploration. This work proposes a multi-fidelity surrogate framework that fuses a small number of high-fidelity orthogonal experiments with a large number of validated low-fidelity CFD–DEM simulations through Co-Kriging. The resulting model predicts breakage performance quickly, returns a calibrated uncertainty for each prediction, and, when coupled with Bayesian optimization, locates optimal parameters with far fewer evaluations than a full-grid search. We first assemble a three-tier dataset from historical experiments, new orthogonal experiments (an L25 design over pressure, abrasive mass flow, standoff distance, and grain size), and validated FLUENT–EDEM simulations, and then benchmark single-fidelity Gaussian-process and gradient-boosting baselines against established semi-empirical formulas. `[PLACEHOLDER: report single-fidelity and multi-fidelity accuracy, cross-condition generalization, uncertainty calibration, and the cost reduction factor of Bayesian optimization.]` The framework provides a transferable and interpretable predictor for AAJ rock breakage and a template for data-efficient surrogate modeling of jet–rock interaction.

**Keywords:** abrasive air jet; rock breakage; multi-fidelity surrogate model; Co-Kriging; Bayesian optimization; machine learning

## 1 Introduction

Abrasive air jets have emerged as an effective tool for coal and rock breakage, gas-drainage enhancement, and TBM-assisted tunnelling [1–4]. Because an air jet carries abrasive particles in a compressible gas rather than in a liquid, its energy transfer and rock-breaking mechanism differ from those of the more widely studied abrasive water jet (AWJ), and its substantially lower water consumption makes it attractive for water-scarce and gas-outburst-prone coal seams [5, 6]. The breakage performance of an AAJ — quantified by erosion depth, erosion volume, or erosion rate — is governed by a strongly coupled set of jet parameters (pressure, abrasive mass flow), abrasive properties (density, grain size, shape), nozzle geometry, and target mechanical properties (uniaxial compressive strength, confinement). The relationships among these variables are nonlinear and non-monotonic; for example, erosion depth and volume first increase and then decrease with standoff distance, and the erosion rate varies parabolically with pressure, peaking near a specific value [7, 8].

Two families of models currently describe these relationships. The first is semi-empirical: several regressions fitted to the team's own experiments, such as the erosion-volume model (relative error < 10%) [9], the cutting-depth model for nuclear-vessel stainless steel (R² = 0.9895) [10], and the cubic polynomial for coal-breakage depth and volume (R² = 0.995) [7]. These formulas are compact and physically interpretable but are fitted on homogeneous conditions; when transferred to a different rock, pressure range, or abrasive, their accuracy degrades markedly. The second family is data-driven: neural networks have been widely applied to predict jet-cutting performance [11–15]. A representative recent study by Jiang et al. [15] built a BP neural network optimized by genetic and particle-swarm algorithms to predict the cutting depth and width of a rotating abrasive water jet across five rock types, achieving average relative errors of 13.3% and 5.4% for depth and width, respectively. This line of work shows that neural networks fit jet–rock interaction well, but it also inherits three limitations: (i) it treats the problem as a single-fidelity black box, requiring hundreds of expensive experiments (≈500 in [15]) to reach usable accuracy; (ii) it provides no uncertainty estimate, so a field operator cannot know when a prediction is trustworthy; and (iii) it does not exploit the physics or the cheaper numerical simulations that the same research groups already use.

Multi-fidelity modeling addresses exactly these limitations [16–18]. It fuses a small amount of expensive high-fidelity data with a large amount of cheap low-fidelity data; for jet–rock interaction, the natural low-fidelity source is the CFD–DEM simulation suite (FLUENT–EDEM for gas–solid, VOF–DEM for liquid–solid) that has been validated against experiments in the literature [8, 19, 20]. Co-Kriging is a well-established multi-fidelity Gaussian-process method that learns the systematic bias between the low- and high-fidelity levels and additionally yields predictive uncertainties [16]. Coupled with Bayesian optimization, the surrogate can then direct further experiments or simulations to the most promising and most uncertain regions, reducing the number of evaluations required to locate an optimum [21, 22].

To the best of our knowledge, multi-fidelity surrogate modeling has not been applied to abrasive air jet rock breakage, and the field still relies on either single-fidelity regression or black-box neural networks. In this work we therefore (i) construct a three-tier dataset — historical experiments (seed/prior), new L25 orthogonal experiments (high-fidelity anchors), and validated FLUENT–EDEM simulations (low-fidelity bulk); (ii) build single-fidelity baselines and a Co-Kriging multi-fidelity surrogate with an energy intermediate variable for interpretability; and (iii) close the loop with Bayesian optimization and report the resulting cost reduction. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 details the data strategy, the orthogonal design, the simulation and validation protocol, and the surrogate models. Section 3 presents the data audit, the single- and multi-fidelity results, the uncertainty analysis, and the optimization case. Section 4 concludes.

## 2 Methods

### 2.1 Overall framework

Figure 1 (placeholder) summarizes the framework. Input features x comprise the jet parameters (pressure p, abrasive mass flow ṁ, standoff distance L, grain size d) and, in later stages, target properties (rock UCS σ, confinement) and nozzle geometry. Outputs y comprise erosion depth H, erosion volume V, erosion rate ER, and specific energy SE. Three data tiers feed the model:

- **Tier 1 — historical data (seed/prior):** approximately 30–60 clean, cross-aligned points extracted from the team's 49 papers and theses, covering extreme conditions and providing the physical priors.
- **Tier 2 — new orthogonal experiments (high-fidelity anchors):** an L25(5⁶) design (Section 2.3) yielding 25 points with all features and all labels plus input energy, on a single target rock to guarantee a homogeneous, fully-labelled anchor set.
- **Tier 3 — validated simulations (low-fidelity bulk):** 500–2000 FLUENT–EDEM points produced only after the template is validated to within 10% of the Tier-2 experiments (Section 2.4).

The modeling pipeline is a Co-Kriging multi-fidelity surrogate (Section 2.5) followed by Bayesian optimization (Section 2.6).

### 2.2 Variable definitions and non-dimensionalization

Table 1 lists the core variables. Because historical experiments span pressures of 2–60 MPa and rock strengths of 6.35–200 MPa, raw concatenation would induce severe distribution drift. We therefore non-dimensionalize before fusion: pressure-to-strength ratio p\* = p/σ, specific-energy breakage volume Vε = V/E_in (the team has calibrated Vε = 48.71125/σ), standoff-to-core-length ratio L\* = L/L_core, and grain-size-to-standoff ratio d\* = d/L.

### 2.3 Orthogonal experimental design (high-fidelity anchors)

We adopt an L25(5⁶) design over five factors at five levels (Table 2). The levels bracket the team's known optima (e.g., optimal pressure ratio n ≈ 1.12, optimal volume fraction ≈ 10%, erosion-rate peak near 15 MPa [7, 8, 19]). The default target is granite (UCS ≈ 135 MPa), matching the "rock breakage" scope and aligning with existing low-pressure granite data; the target can be switched to coal if the permeability-enhancement line is prioritized. Every point records erosion depth and volume, input energy (for Vε), the rock UCS of the same specimen batch, and, optionally, the abrasive velocity for simulation validation.

| Table 2 — L25 factors | Symbol | Suggested levels |
|---|---|---|
| jet pressure | p (MPa) | 2, 5, 10, 15, 25 |
| abrasive mass flow | ṁ (g/s) | 8, 16, 32, 50, 75 |
| standoff distance | L (mm) | 5 levels around the team's optimal-standoff curve |
| grain size | d (μm) | 5 levels starting from ≈80 mesh |
| erosion time (or traverse speed) | t (s) / Vs | 3, 5, 10, 15, 20 s (breakage) / traverse speed (cutting) |

The rationale for an orthogonal rather than a full-factorial design is economy: 25 points cover five factors and are achievable in 1–2 weeks, consistent with the team's prior orthogonal designs [10, 15]. These 25 homogeneous, fully-labelled points are more valuable to the surrogate than the 30–60 heterogeneous historical points because they are same-source, reproducible, and support the cross-condition generalization claim.

### 2.4 Numerical simulation (low-fidelity bulk) and validation

The low-fidelity arm uses FLUENT with the RNG k–ε model and a discrete-phase model (DPM), coupled with EDEM for particle–particle and particle–wall interactions (FLUENT–EDEM), which is the standard gas–solid tool for AAJ acceleration and erosion in this field [8, 20]. We do not use VOF–DEM here because it models a liquid carrier (abrasive water jet) rather than the gas carrier of AAJs [19]. The workflow is strictly "validate first, then bulk": (1) reproduce the 25 Tier-2 experiments with the FLUENT–EDEM template and require <10% error on depth and erosion rate; (2) only after passing validation, sweep 500–2000 points (p × ṁ × L × d) by Latin hypercube or dense grid sampling. The simulations output the particle velocity field and the target-face kinetic-energy density, which we condense into an energy intermediate scalar — ρEag and naj as defined in [19], or E = E_K/S as defined in [20] — used as an interpretable intermediate variable (Section 2.5).

### 2.5 Co-Kriging multi-fidelity surrogate

Single-fidelity baselines are a Gaussian-process regressor (RBF + white kernel, which natively returns predictive variance) and gradient-boosted trees (GradientBoosting / XGBoost), both trained on Tier 1 + Tier 2 data, with 5-fold cross-validation reporting MAE, RMSE, and R². The baselines are compared against the semi-empirical formulas of [7, 9, 10].

The core model is Co-Kriging [16]. Let the low-fidelity field and high-fidelity field be

$$ y_L(x) = f_L(x) + \varepsilon_L, \qquad y_H(x) = \rho\, y_L(x) + \delta(x), $$

with f_L and δ independent Gaussian processes and ε_L observational noise. The joint covariance of the high-fidelity field is then ρ²k_L + k_δ. The low-fidelity arm is the 500–2000 validated simulation points; the high-fidelity arm is the 25 orthogonal points plus the aligned historical points. The model learns both the scale ρ and the bias process δ(x) that captures the systematic simulation-to-experiment discrepancy. To avoid a black-box end-to-end mapping, we structure the predictor in two stages: parameters → energy intermediate (ρEag / naj / E) → breakage output, so the energy bottleneck is physically interpretable.

Physical priors are encoded as constraints where the data are sparse: the monotonic increase of the optimal mass flow with pressure and traverse speed, the convex (parabolic) erosion-rate–pressure relationship, and the inverse specific-energy scaling Vε ∝ 1/σ [7, 8, 19]. These are implemented either as constrained-GP or PINN-style residual terms to regularize the small-sample regime.

### 2.6 Bayesian optimization and active learning

We use BoTorch with q-expected-improvement to optimize either the erosion volume (maximize) or the specific energy (minimize), subject to a kerf-quality constraint (width-to-depth ratio P ≤ 0.5, the narrow-deep kerf criterion from [23]). At each iteration the acquisition function selects the point with the highest expected improvement and uncertainty; only that point is evaluated by simulation or experiment, for 10–20 iterations. The cost reduction is reported as the ratio M/N between a full-grid search (M evaluations) and the number of evaluations the optimizer used (N), with an expected 5–20× reduction.
